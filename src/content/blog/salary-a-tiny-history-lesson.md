Ask for a raise and, without knowing it, you're negotiating over salt.

## A soldier's salt money

*Salary* comes from the Latin *salarium*, built on *sal* — "salt." The standard story is that Roman soldiers received part of their pay as an allowance earmarked for buying salt, a genuinely precious commodity before refrigeration, used to preserve meat and fish through the year. Historians argue about how literally to take that (some think it was simply pay *associated with* the salt trade, not literal salt money), but either way the word locked salt and wages together permanently. Every time someone discusses their salary, they're using a word built around a substance so valuable it once shaped Roman logistics and taxation.

## Ordinary words, buried machinery

Once you notice one word like this, you start seeing them everywhere — ordinary vocabulary quietly preserving a piece of technology, belief, or daily life that's otherwise long gone.

- **Clue** comes from *clew*, an old word for a ball of thread — a direct nod to the myth of Theseus, who used a ball of thread to find his way back out of the Minotaur's labyrinth. "Following a clue" is, etymologically, following a thread.
- **Disaster** splits into Latin *dis-* ("bad") and *astro* ("star") — a *disaster* was literally a bad star, from an age when catastrophes were blamed on unfavorable planetary alignments.
- **Companion** comes from Latin *com-* ("together") + *panis* ("bread") — a companion was, at the root, someone you shared bread with.

None of these etymologies are decoration. They're compressed history: a soldier's ration, a hero's escape route, an astrologer's excuse, a shared meal — all still riding along silently inside words we use without a second thought.

## Why it's worth noticing

You don't need to know any of this to use the words correctly. That's exactly what makes it interesting: the history survives whether or not anyone remembers it, fossilized in sound and spelling long after the beliefs and practices behind it have disappeared. Say "salary" once with this in mind, and it stops being a neutral HR term and starts being a two-thousand-year-old paycheck.
