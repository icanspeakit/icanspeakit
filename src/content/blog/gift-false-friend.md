Tell a German speaker "here's a little Gift for you" in English and, if they know both languages, you'll get a nervous laugh. In German, *Gift* means poison.

## Same word, one very dark turn

Both words descend from the same Proto-Germanic root, *geftiz*, meaning simply "a giving" — related to the verb "to give." English kept that meaning almost unchanged: a gift is something given.

German took a detour. By the medieval period, German had started using *Gift* as a polite, euphemistic translation of the Latin *dosis* — literally "a giving" — which physicians used for a dose of medicine. Medieval medicine leaned heavily on substances that were medicine in small amounts and lethal in large ones (arsenic and mercury compounds among them), so "a dose" and "a poison" lived very close together in everyday speech. Over time, in German specifically, the euphemism won out completely and *Gift* narrowed to mean only the dangerous kind of dose.

English medicine borrowed the same Latin idea but kept the word "dose" instead of building it out of "gift," so the two languages' words for "giving" ended up on opposite sides of a life-or-death line.

## It gets messier

German still keeps a fossil of the old, neutral meaning in a few compound words — *Mitgift* (dowry, literally "with-gift") and *Wortgift* aren't real slurs, but you can see the shadow of "a giving" if you know where to look. Meanwhile Scandinavian languages split the difference: Swedish *gift* can mean either "married" or "poison," depending entirely on context, which is its own small trap for beginners.

## Why this happens so often

"False friends" — words that look related and clearly are related, but have drifted into different meanings — are extremely common between closely related languages, precisely *because* they share so much vocabulary to begin with. Distantly related languages rarely trick you this way; it's the close cousins, like English and German, or Spanish and Italian, that do it best.
