Every other topic on this site is about the mechanics you can practice your way through — a sound, a stress pattern, a pause. Fluency disorders aren't that, and it's worth being direct about why before pointing anywhere else.

## An accent is a system. A fluency disorder isn't.

A foreign accent is a *consistent, rule-governed* substitution — a whole system for turning the sounds of one language into the sounds of another. It's why a French speaker's English "th" reliably becomes a "z" or a "s" sound: it's not random, it's a predictable mapping between two phonological systems. Learn the mapping, drill the individual contrasts, and the accent softens. That's what a minimal-pairs exercise or a shadowing drill actually trains.

Stuttering (repeating or blocking on sounds — "I-I-I want to go") and cluttering (speech that's rushed, irregular, and hard to follow, often with disorganized phrasing) aren't mismatches between two sound systems. They're disruptions in the timing and motor planning of speech production itself, in the speaker's *native* language as much as any other. A stutter doesn't reliably map "b" to some other sound the way an accent maps "th" to "z" — the same speaker might glide through "b" a hundred times and then block hard on it once, with no accent-style pattern to drill against.

## Why that matters for what this site can (and can't) do

Everything else here works by giving you a target sound, letting you hear a model, and letting you compare your own attempt against it — a format that only works because the target is stable and repeatable. Fluency disorders don't have that kind of fixed target. What helps varies by person, often changes over time, and frequently involves things well outside sound production — breathing coordination, motor planning, sometimes the emotional weight of anticipating a block. That's clinical territory, assessed and treated by speech-language pathologists (SLPs), not something a self-checking drill can responsibly stand in for.

## Where to actually go

If stuttering or cluttering is something you or someone you know deals with, the useful next step isn't a worksheet — it's a licensed speech-language pathologist, ideally one with specific experience in fluency disorders. In the US, the [American Speech-Language-Hearing Association](https://www.asha.org/) maintains a directory (ASHA ProFind). In the UK, the [Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists](https://www.rcslt.org/) does the same. [The Stuttering Foundation](https://www.stutteringfoundation.org/) is also a solid, long-running resource specifically for stuttering, with referral help and free materials.

The rest of this site is still a good place to work on the parts of speech that *are* drillable — the sounds, the stress, the rhythm. This one just isn't one of them, and pretending otherwise would do more harm than a missing feature ever could.
