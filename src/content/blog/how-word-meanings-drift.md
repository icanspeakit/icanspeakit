Call someone "nice" today and it's a mild compliment. Call them "nice" in the 1300s and you'd be calling them an idiot.

## From ignorant to pleasant

*Nice* comes from Latin *nescius*, "ignorant" — literally *ne-* ("not") plus *scire* ("to know"), the same root that gives English "science." It entered English through Old French still carrying that sense of foolishness, and for a couple of centuries "nice" meant something close to "silly" or "simple-minded." From there it drifted through an odd sequence of meanings — "fussy," then "precise" (a "nice distinction" still means a fine, exact one), then "refined," and only by the 18th century did it settle into today's soft, all-purpose "pleasant."

## It's a whole pattern, not one word

Linguists call this kind of shift *amelioration* (meanings improving over time) and its opposite, *pejoration* (meanings souring). English is full of both:

- **Awful** used to mean "inspiring awe" — a purely positive or reverent word, describing something like a cathedral or a storm. Over time it narrowed to focus only on the frightening side of awe, and eventually just meant "bad."
- **Silly** comes from Old English *sælig*, meaning "blessed" or "happy" — related to German *selig*. It drifted through "innocent," then "pitiable," before landing on today's "foolish."
- **Egregious** once meant "distinguished" or "outstandingly good" (from Latin *e-* "out of" + *grex* "the herd" — literally "standing out from the flock"). It flipped to its modern negative sense, "outstandingly bad," by the 1500s.

Same mechanism, opposite directions — words don't drift toward "better" or "worse" on principle, they just drift, and which way depends on how people actually use them.

## Why words flip at all

A few forces do most of the work: overuse wears a word's original force down (an "awesome" burger is not literally awe-inspiring), euphemism pulls polite words toward taboo meanings until they need replacing, and irony calcifies into a new literal sense if enough people use a word sarcastically for long enough. None of it is centrally planned — no committee decided "nice" should soften from "foolish" to "pleasant." Millions of individual conversations nudged it there, one slightly-off usage at a time, until the old meaning was gone and nobody noticed it leave.
